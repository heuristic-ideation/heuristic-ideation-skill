# Direct Manipulation (H13-H15)

When you already have a proposition, these heuristics are designed to break conventional thinking patterns. Rather than accepting problems as presented, deliberately transform ideas to reveal hidden possibilities and challenge assumptions.

**Usage Note**: Be selective—choose the most relevant heuristic and develop ideas thoroughly. Quality and depth are more important than using multiple heuristics or generating many ideas.

## H13: Challenge Core Assumptions

**Core Principle:** Consider circumstances where the opposite of a familiar assumption or a reverse in causal direction might be true.

**Application:** Every theory and problem contains implicit assumptions. By making these explicit and challenging them, researchers can discover new explanatory frameworks and unlock innovative solutions. This includes questioning causal direction, scope conditions, and taken-for-granted beliefs.

**Key Questions:**
- What assumptions underlie this theory or problem?
- What if the opposite were true?
- What if causation runs in the reverse direction?
- Under what conditions might this assumption fail?
- What would happen if we removed this constraint?
- What are we taking for granted?

**Examples:**
- **Physics:** Einstein's special relativity challenged Newton's assumptions of absolute space and time, proposing instead that both are relative to the observer's frame of reference
  - *Hypothesis*: "The speed of light will remain constant for all observers regardless of their relative motion, and time dilation will increase with velocity"
- **Economics:** Challenging the assumption of rational actors led to behavioral economics
  - *Hypothesis*: "Decision-makers will systematically deviate from rational choice when choices are framed as losses rather than gains, even when outcomes are identical"
- **Medicine:** Questioning that bacteria couldn't survive in stomach acid led to discovery of H. pylori and ulcer treatment
  - *Hypothesis*: "Gastric ulcers are caused by bacterial infection rather than stress, and will respond to antibiotic treatment"
- **Neuroscience:** Challenging assumption that adult brains don't generate new neurons led to neurogenesis research
  - *Hypothesis*: "The adult hippocampus will show evidence of newly generated neurons that contribute to learning and memory formation"
- **Machine Learning:** Questioning assumption that more data always improves performance revealed diminishing returns and data quality importance
  - *Hypothesis*: "Beyond a critical dataset size, model performance gains will be greater from improving data quality than from adding more low-quality data"

**Implementation Strategy:**

### 1. Identify Core Assumptions
- Write down the central claims of your theory/problem
- For each claim, ask: "What must be true for this to hold?"
- List implicit assumptions explicitly
- Distinguish empirical from logical assumptions

### 2. Systematically Challenge Each Assumption
- **Reversal:** What if the opposite were true?
- **Boundary testing:** Under what conditions does this fail?
- **Causation flip:** What if B causes A instead of A causes B?
- **Scope questioning:** Does this apply universally or only in specific contexts?

### 3. Explore the Inverted World
- Develop what the theory/problem looks like with challenged assumption
- Identify new predictions
- Search for evidence that supports the alternative
- Determine if the challenge opens new research directions

### 4. Synthesize
- Can both the original and challenged assumption be partially true?
- Are there moderating conditions determining which holds?
- Does the challenge reveal a more general principle?

**Types of Assumptions to Challenge:**
- **Causal Direction:** "Does X cause Y, or could Y cause X?"
- **Linearity:** "Is the relationship linear or nonlinear?"
- **Universality:** "Does this apply everywhere or only in specific contexts?"
- **Necessity:** "Is this factor necessary or just common?"
- **Sufficiency:** "Is this factor sufficient or just contributory?"
- **Temporal Order:** "Must A precede B or could they be simultaneous?"

**Common Domains for Assumption Challenging:**
- Causation vs. correlation
- Direct vs. indirect effects
- Necessary vs. sufficient conditions
- Universal vs. conditional relationships
- Active agent vs. passive recipient roles

**Historical Examples:**
- **Heliocentrism:** Challenging Earth-centered universe assumption
- **Germ Theory:** Challenging miasma theory of disease
- **Plate Tectonics:** Challenging assumption of fixed continents
- **Quantum Mechanics:** Challenging deterministic universe assumption

## H14: Probe the System with Controlled Changes

**Core Principle:** Test a system's behavior to small, targeted perturbations as well as large, boundary-testing changes.

**Application:** Systems reveal their nature when stressed or altered. By systematically varying parameters, introducing perturbations, or exploring extreme conditions, researchers can map system boundaries, identify critical thresholds, and uncover hidden mechanisms.

**Key Questions:**
- What happens if we change this parameter slightly?
- What happens at extreme values?
- Where are the tipping points or thresholds?
- What changes system behavior qualitatively?
- What remains constant despite changes?
- What breaks the system?

**Examples:**
- **Pharmacology:** Dose-response curves revealing therapeutic windows and toxicity thresholds
- **Psychology:** Studying behavior under stress to understand coping mechanisms
- **Economics:** Examining market behavior during crises vs. normal times
- **Ecology:** Manipulating predator populations to understand ecosystem resilience
- **Materials Science:** Testing materials at extreme temperatures/pressures to understand failure modes

**Implementation Strategy:**

### 1. Identify Manipulable Parameters
- What variables can be controlled?
- What ranges are feasible?
- What changes are ethical/safe?

### 2. Design Systematic Variations
**Small Perturbations:**
- Vary one parameter at a time
- Use small increments
- Map local behavior
- Identify sensitivity

**Large Boundary-Pushing:**
- Test extreme values
- Combine multiple changes
- Push to system limits
- Identify breaking points

### 3. Observe System Response
- Quantitative changes (magnitude)
- Qualitative changes (character)
- Threshold effects
- Interaction effects
- Resilience and recovery

### 4. Map the System
- Create parameter space maps
- Identify stability regions
- Locate phase transitions
- Chart feedback loops
- Document boundary conditions

**Types of Perturbations:**
- **Parametric:** Varying continuous variables
- **Structural:** Changing system components
- **Environmental:** Altering external conditions
- **Temporal:** Varying timing or duration
- **Stochastic:** Introducing randomness

**Experimental Designs:**
- **Factorial Designs:** Testing multiple parameters simultaneously
- **Dose-Response:** Systematic parameter scaling
- **Stress Tests:** Pushing to extremes
- **Ablation Studies:** Removing components
- **Sensitivity Analysis:** Identifying critical parameters

**What to Look For:**
- **Linear vs. Nonlinear:** Does doubling input double output?
- **Thresholds:** Do effects suddenly appear/disappear?
- **Hysteresis:** Does history matter?
- **Resilience:** How quickly does system recover?
- **Emergence:** Do new properties appear at extremes?

**Safety and Ethics:**
- Consider risks of extreme manipulations
- Use simulation when direct testing is dangerous
- Protect participants/environment
- Have contingency plans

## H15: Reconfigure the Conceptual Framework

**Core Principle:** Alter your idea's internal structure by reframing its core components like variables and interactions.

**Application:** By deliberately restructuring how we conceptualize a problem, we can discover hidden relationships, alternative explanations, and new research questions. This includes adding moderators, removing presumed effects, changing levels of analysis, or redefining boundaries.

**Key Questions:**
- What if we added a moderating variable?
- What happens if we remove a supposedly critical factor?
- What if we changed the level of analysis?
- How would the framework look from a different perspective?
- What if we redrew the system boundaries?
- What relationships have we not considered?

**Examples:**
- **Psychology:** Adding cultural context as moderator to reveal that Western psychology findings don't universalize
- **Economics:** Shifting from individual to household level of analysis changing consumption theories
- **Epidemiology:** Removing assumption that disease spreads uniformly, leading to network models
- **Climate Science:** Expanding boundaries to include feedback loops previously treated as external
- **Sociology:** Reconceptualizing social class from categorical to continuous variable revealing gradient effects

**Implementation Strategy:**

### 1. Map Current Framework
- Identify key variables
- Diagram relationships
- Note assumed boundaries
- List implicit inclusions/exclusions
- Specify level of analysis

### 2. Apply Transformations

**Add Elements:**
- **Moderators:** "Under what conditions does this relationship hold?"
- **Mediators:** "What's the process linking cause and effect?"
- **Controls:** "What confounds have we missed?"
- **Context:** "What environmental factors matter?"

**Remove Elements:**
- "What if we removed this 'essential' component?"
- "Could the system function without this?"
- "What would simpler version reveal?"

**Change Relationships:**
- Linear → Nonlinear
- Direct → Mediated
- Unidirectional → Bidirectional
- Additive → Interactive

**Shift Levels:**
- Individual → Group
- Micro → Macro
- Molecular → Cellular → Organismal
- Short-term → Long-term

**Redefine Boundaries:**
- Expand to include previously external factors
- Narrow to focus on core mechanisms
- Shift temporal boundaries
- Change spatial scale

### 3. Evaluate New Configuration
- What new predictions emerge?
- What previously unexplained patterns now make sense?
- What new questions arise?
- Is this configuration more parsimonious or more complex?
- Does it integrate previously separate findings?

### 4. Test Empirically
- Design studies testing new configuration
- Compare predictive power to original
- Look for evidence supporting new relationships
- Identify boundary conditions

**Specific Techniques:**

**Moderator Analysis:**
- "The effect of X on Y depends on Z"
- Reveals when/where/for whom effects occur
- Example: Drug efficacy varies by genotype

**Mediator Analysis:**
- "X affects Y through M"
- Reveals process or mechanism
- Example: Education affects health through health literacy

**Level Shifting:**
- Individual properties don't always aggregate
- Emergent properties at higher levels
- Example: Individual neurons vs. neural networks

**Boundary Redrawing:**
- Include feedback loops
- Incorporate context
- Extend time horizons
- Example: Ecosystem boundaries including human activity

**Perspective Shifting:**
- View problem from different stakeholder positions
- Use different theoretical lenses
- Example: Mental health from medical vs. social model

**Common Reconfigurations:**
- Adding interaction terms
- Introducing time lags
- Considering bidirectional effects
- Expanding to multiple levels
- Including nonlinearities
- Incorporating stochasticity

## Using These Heuristics Together

These three manipulation heuristics work synergistically to transform problems:

1. **H13** challenges what we believe to be true
2. **H14** tests what happens when we change things
3. **H15** restructures how we organize our understanding

**Workflow Example:**
1. Challenge assumption that X causes Y (H13)
2. Probe the system by varying X to see actual effects (H14)
3. Reconfigure framework to include bidirectional effects and moderators revealed by probing (H15)
4. Challenge new assumptions in refined framework (H13)

**Integration with Other Categories:**
- These heuristics often apply to ideas generated from observation (H1-H3)
- They help resolve conflicts in literature (H4)
- They guide what to test in pilots (H11) and simulations (H12)
- They lead to formal models (H18) that can be rigorously tested

This category represents active, interventionist thinking. Rather than passively accepting problems as given, these heuristics empower researchers to deliberately reshape ideas, revealing possibilities that conventional framing conceals. They are particularly powerful when initial ideas feel stuck or when existing frameworks fail to explain observations.
